The purpose of this study is to assess the potential interference from the usage of MSS for direct-to-
device applications into IMT operating in the frequency bands 1 920-1 980 MHz and 2 110-2
170 MHz. These frequency bands are subsets of the frequency band 1 710-2 200 MHz.
A4.2.1.1 Study A
This study assesses the interference from space-to-Earth transmissions of non-geostationary (non-
GSO) DC-MSS-IMT systems into terrestrial IMT base station (BS) and user equipment (UE)
receivers operating in the frequency bands 1 920-1 980 MHz and 2 110-2 170 MHz, respectively,
considering different use cases and simulation scenarios.
The basic sharing scenario between DC-MSS-IMT and IMT is presented in Figure A4.2.1.1-1,
extracted and adapted from document 4C/356, which illustrates a generic scenario involving the
operation of a DC-MSS-IMT system and a terrestrial IMT (TN-IMT) system near international
borders, where neighboring countries use the same frequency in adjacent geographical areas for
different systems. In this situation, TN-IMT stations within one country may be subject to potential
interference from satellites belonging to the DC-MSS-IMT system operating in neighboring nations.

Protection Criterion
With respect to protection criteria for terrestrial IMT (TN-IMT), studies are still ongoing under the
coordination of Working Party 5D. Results from some of these studies have recently been compiled
in Annex 4.6 to Document 5D/792 (WP5D Chair’s Report).
In this compatibility study, the interference-to-noise ratio (INR) of -6 dB is considered as the
protection criterion for terrestrial IMT according to the Report ITU-R M.2292. This protection
criterion for the IMT BS and UE receivers is a widely accepted criterion within ITU-R studies for
assessing the impact of long-term aggregate interference into digital mobile systems, corresponding
to a 1 dB rise in the receiver noise floor.
It should be noted that while this value is used as the basis for the present study, the definitive
protection criteria for IMT systems under Agenda Item 1.13 are still under consideration within the
responsible group, Working Party 5D, and have not yet been formally agreed upon.

To model a realistic operational approach for managing cross-border interference, this analysis
incorporates a distance from the political borders (Figure A4.2.1.1-5), which will be named in this
document as operational margin. This margin defines a distance from the border within the territories
of Brazil and Argentina where the center of the DC-MSS satellite beams shall not be pointed. This
operational constraint creates a beam-pointing exclusion zone, ensuring that the high-gain main lobe
of any satellite beam is directed only toward the interior of the licensed country. As a result, the
interference received by the victim IMT station in Paraguay is primarily composed of side-lobe
emissions, representing a practical mitigation technique that DC-MSS operators could implement.
This scenario was specifically chosen to evaluate the aggregate interference from multiple DC-MSS-
IMT systems operating in neighboring countries into the territory of a third country where the service
is not deployed, representing a realistic challenge for cross-border coordination.
All simulations presented in this study were conducted using the SHARC (SHARing and

replication of results at the following web address: https://github.com/Radio-Spectrum/SHARC.

A4.2.1.1.4.2 Non-GSO Constellation Modelling
The NGSO constellations were modelled based on their defined Keplerian elements, including
altitude, inclination, and the number of orbital planes and the number of satellites per plane.
To perform the statistical analysis independent of a specific time epoch, a snapshot-based Monte
Carlo methodology was employed. In this approach, instead of propagating the satellite positions over
a time-based simulation, the initial orbital angles for each satellite in the constellation were
randomized. Specifically, a reference satellite and orbital plane are defined. The mean anomaly (M)
of this satellite within its orbital plane and the right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN, Ω) of
that orbital plane (Figure A4.2.1.18) are modeled as independent random variables uniformly
distributed over [0°, 360°]. In each snapshot of the Monte Carlo process, the values of M and Ω are
randomly drawn, and the orbital positions of the remaining satellites and orbital planes are then
determined in accordance with the orbital parameters of the DCMSSIMT system, as listed in
Table A.4.2.1.11.

This method generates a statistically representative snapshot of the entire constellation’s geometry at

total of 10,000 snapshots was generated in this study, providing a sufficient sample size for statistical
convergence.
A4.2.1.1.4.3 Satellite Beam Generation and Pointing Methodology
FIGURE A4.2.1.1-9
Satellite beam assignment
To model the service deployment for the DC-MSS-IMT systems, a Service Grid methodology was
employed for the generation and pointing of satellite beams (Figures A.4.2.1.1-5 and A.4.2.1.1-9).
This method ensures that continuous coverage is provided throughout the intended service area while
adhering to operational constraints.
The process is defined by the following steps:
1. Service Grid Generation
The defined service area is tessellated into a grid of contiguous and non-overlapping hexagonal-cells
(Figure A.4.2.1.1-9), whose centers are illustrated in Figure A.4.2.1.1-5. The radius of each
hexagonal-cell is set to be equal to the coverage radius of a single satellite beam, R, shown in Figure
A4.2.1.1-4, ensuring that one beam can effectively serve one hexagonal-cell. These hexagonal
structures were used for illustrative purposes, with R, as described earlier in this document, being the
most relevant element of the proposed methodology. In this study, R is set to 40.448 km and 26.195
km for the analysis of System 3, at altitudes of 525 km and 340 km, respectively, following the -7 dB
contour of the radiation pattern of each beam.

3. Beam Assignment and Pointing
For each hexagonal-cell within the defined service area, a single beam is assigned based on the best
server principle. The satellite with the highest elevation angle as seen from the center of that
hexagonal cell is selected to provide service. This satellite then points the main lobe of one of its
beams directly to the center of the assigned hexagon. This methodology ensures that the entire service
area is systematically covered by the assigned beams, utilizing the satellite with the most favorable
link geometry for each cell (Figure A.4.2.1.1-10).
The satellite beam size is defined by its average service contour at the 7 dB power falloff point from
the peak gain of the main lobe. This definition is used to implement an exclusive beam layout, where
adjacent beams are placed no closer than their respective 7 dB contours. This 7 dB value must be
derived using the specific antenna radiation pattern described in model 1.4 of Recommendation ITU-
R S.1528.

A4.2.1.1.5 Results of the study
This study addresses a cross-border scenario involving three South American countries (Brazil,
Argentina, and Paraguay) that use the same frequency band, with two countries covered by the DC-
MSS-IMT system and the third operating a terrestrial IMT system (TN-IMT). The victim system

As previously mentioned, DC-MSS-IMT system 3 is considered in the study. Two altitude levels for
system 3 are employed for comparison purposes. The operational margin defined in a previous section
is also varied to assess the impact of this interference mitigation strategy. Finally, the study includes
an analysis of the DC-MSS-IMT system load factor, assuming values of 20% and 50%.

covering that region, thus increasing the potential for aggregate interference when the IMT-BS is
pointing in that direction.

A4.2.1.1.6 Conclusion of study A
The results of this study show that sharing between the TN-IMT system operating in the downlink
and the DC-MSS-IMT system operating also in the downlink, in the frequency band 2 110-2 170
MHz, is feasible under in-band interference conditions, provided that an appropriate interference
mitigation strategy is employed. This study demonstrated that a mitigation strategy based on defining
an operational margin, which limits the area of incidence of the satellite beams from the DC-MSS-
IMT system, can be effective in mitigating interference, allowing compliance with the TN-IMT
protection criterion under conditions of low altitude and reasonable load factor.
On the other hand, the results obtained in this study showed that spectrum sharing between the TN-
IMT system operating in the uplink and the DC-MSS-IMT system operating in the downlink, in the
frequency band 1 920 - 1 980 MHz, demonstrates that with the application of an operational margin
along national borders, DC-MSS-IMT systems can coexist with incumbent IMT networks under the
considered protection criterion. The results confirm that interference levels remain below the INR
threshold when operational load factors are kept at or below 10% for systems at 340 km altitude, and
at or below 20% for systems at 525 km altitude. These findings highlight that appropriate system
design and traffic management are essential to ensure compatibility, and they provide a clear
indication of the operating conditions under which DC-MSS-IMT deployment could be feasible
without causing harmful interference to IMT services.
