A4.3 Adjacent-band studies
A4.3.1 Compatibility of FS in the frequency bands 2 200-2 290 and DC-MSS-IMT operating
in the frequency band 2 110-2 200 MHz (downlink)
The purpose of this study is to assess the potential interference from the usage of MSS for direct-to-
device (DC-MSS-IMT) applications into Frequency Service (FS) operating in the frequency bands 2
200-2 290 MHz. These frequency band is adjacent to the frequency band 1 710-2 200 MHz scenarios.
A4.3.1.1 Study A
This study assesses the interference from space-to-Earth transmissions of non-geostationary (NGSO)
DC-MSS-IMT systems into Fixed Service (FS) receiver operating in the frequency bands 2 200-2
290 MHz, considering different use cases and simulation scenarios.
The initial compatibility scenario between DC-MSS-IMT and FS, referred to in this study as Case I,
is shown in Figure A4.3.1.1-1, adapted from document 4C/356. It depicts a generic case where a DC-
MSS-IMT system and an FS system operate near international borders, with neighboring countries
utilizing adjacent frequency bands in adjacent regions for different services. In this context, FS
stations in one country may experience potential interference from satellites of the DC-MSS-IMT
system operating in nearby nations.

Protection Criterion
Protection criteria for FS systems below 3 GHz are addressed in Recommendations ITU-R F.1094,
F.1565 and F.1108, and summarized in F.758. For the 30 MHz-3 GHz range, a –6 dB criterion may
apply where the risk of simultaneous interference from other co-primary allocations is negligible;
otherwise, a more stringent value (–6 dB or –10 dB as the maximum aggregate I/N from all co-
primary services) may be required. The cited Recommendations and Document 4C/356 do not specify
which stricter value should be applied to emissions from the DC-MSS-IMT system under continental
coverage. Therefore, this study applies –6 dB as the protection criterion. Should this value be revised,
analyses and conclusions can be updated accordingly, while the presented results remain valid.
In compatibility studies, long-term interference is defined as exceeding 20% of the time in the victim
system (per ITU-R F.758). Thus, this study adopts I/N = –6 dB, not to be exceeded for more than
20% of the time, with 'time' defined by events in the Monte Carlo procedure.
Recommendation ITU-R F.758 states that both long-term and short-term protection criteria should be
defined for FS systems, with short-term interference corresponding to high interference levels
occurring less than 1% of the time. While Annex 1 of F.758 references procedures and examples for
short-term protection (ITU-R F.1494, F.1495, F.1606), none address frequencies below 3 GHz.
Therefore, this compatibility study does not adopt a short-term protection criterion; if such a criterion
is defined in the future, relevant analyses and conclusions can be adjusted accordingly.

A4.3.1.1.4 Methodology
A4.3.1.1.4.1 Interference Scenario
As mentioned, this study analyzes, as Case I, a specific cross-border interference scenario, which is
a concrete and representative implementation of the generalized case described in the working
document. For this analysis, a hypothetical deployment is assumed where a DC-MSS-IMT system is
fully operational, providing service over Brazil’s and Argentina’s territories. The victim under
analysis comprises an FS receiver, located in Paraguay and positioned at a representative point along
its border with Brazil.
As Case II, all South American countries are covered by the DC-MSS-IMT system, including
Paraguay, and an FS system receiver is positioned at the same location as the victim receiver in Case
I. In both cases, the DC-MSS-IMT system and the FS system operate in adjacent frequency band, in
an adjacent-band interference scenario. The two study cases were designed to allow analyses of
compatibility feasibility, with Case I representing a lower-interference context relative to Case II.
Case I also includes an interference mitigation feature, which will be defined later as an operational
margin, or service margin.
To model a realistic operational approach for managing cross-border interference, this analysis
incorporates a service margin from the political borders (Figures A4.3.1.1-7), in study Case I. This
margin defines a distance from the border within the territories of Brazil and Argentina where the
center of the DC-MSS satellite beams shall not be pointed. This operational constraint creates a beam-
pointing exclusion zone, ensuring that the high-gain main lobe of any satellite beam is directed only
toward the interior of the licensed country. As a result, the interference received by the victim IMT

A4.3.1.1.4.3 Non-GSO Constellation Modelling
The NGSO constellations were modelled based on their defined Keplerian elements, including
altitude, inclination, and the number of orbital planes and the number of satellites per plane.
To perform the statistical analysis independent of a specific time epoch, a snapshot-based Monte
Carlo methodology was employed. In this approach, instead of propagating the satellite positions over
a time-based simulation, the initial orbital angles for each satellite in the constellation were
randomized. Specifically, a reference satellite and orbital plane are defined. The mean anomaly (M)
of this satellite within its orbital plane and the right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN, Ω) of
that orbital plane (Figure A4.3.1.1-11) are modeled as independent random variables uniformly
distributed over [0°, 360°]. In each snapshot of the Monte Carlo process, the values of ω and Ω are
randomly drawn, and the orbital positions of the remaining satellites and orbital planes are then
determined in accordance with the orbital parameters of the DC-MSS-IMT system, as listed in
Table A4.3.1.1-1.

A4.3.1.1.4.3 Satellite Beam Generation and Pointing Methodology
FIGURE A4.3.1.1-12
Satellite beam assignment
To model the service deployment for the DC-MSS-IMT systems, a Service Grid methodology was
employed for the generation and pointing of satellite beams (Figures A.4.3.1.1-7, 8, and 12). This
method ensures that continuous coverage is provided throughout the intended service area while
adhering to operational constraints.
The process is defined by the following steps:
1. Service Grid Generation
The defined service area is tessellated into a grid of contiguous and non-overlapping hexagonal-cells
(Figure A.4.3.1.1-12), whose centers are illustrated in Figures A.4.3.1.1-7 and 8, for Study Case I and
II. The radius of each hexagonal-cell is set to be equal to the coverage radius of a single satellite
beam, R, shown in Figure A4.3.1.1-5, ensuring that one beam can effectively serve one hexagonal-
cell. These hexagonal structures were used for illustrative purposes, with R, as described earlier in
this document, being the most relevant element of the proposed methodology. In this study, R is set
to [36.16, 36.25] km and [23.42, 23.48] km for the analysis of System 3, at altitudes of 525 km and
340 km, respectively, following the –7 dB contour of the radiation pattern of each beam, for frequency
[2 197.5, 2 187.5] MHz.
2. Definition of the Service Area
The service area, which comprises all the (hexagonal-)cells eligible to be served, is defined at each
snapshot by a set of operational and geographical constraints. A satellite beam can only be placed
within a cell if the following three conditions are met simultaneously:
 The hexagon is located entirely within the political borders of the country licensed to operate
the service (e.g., Brazil or Argentina, for Study Case I, or all South American countries, for
Study Case II), respecting a pre-defined operational margin from the international border
(Figure A.4.3.1.1-7), in the case of Study I. In this latter case, this creates a beam-pointing
exclusion zone to mitigate cross-border interference. In Study Case II, as the DC-MSS-IMT
system covers all South American countries and the victim FS system may be located in any

 The hexagonal-cell is within the visibility footprint of at least one satellite in the constellation.
 The satellite whose beam covers a cell is visible from the specific victim ground station under
study, allowing for the calculation of the interference path. Visibility is defined by a minimum
elevation angle, which in this study was set to 5 degrees, in accordance with Document
4C/356.
3. Beam Assignment and Pointing
For each hexagonal-cell within the defined service area, a single beam is assigned based on the best
server principle. The satellite with the highest elevation angle as seen from the center of that
hexagonal cell is selected to provide service. This satellite then points the main lobe of one of its
beams directly to the center of the assigned hexagon. This methodology ensures that the entire service
area is systematically covered by the assigned beams, utilizing the satellite with the most favorable
link geometry for each cell (Figure A.4.3.1.1-13 (a) and (b)).
The satellite beam size is defined by its average service contour at the -7 dB power falloff point from
the peak gain of the main lobe. This definition is used to implement an exclusive beam layout, where
adjacent beams are placed no closer than their respective 7 dB contours. This 7 dB value must be
derived using the specific antenna radiation pattern described in model 1.4 of Recommendation ITU-
R S.1528.

A4.3.1.1.6 Conclusion of study A
Two case studies were addressed in this contribution: 1) DC-MSS-IMT coverage of two South
American countries under adjacent-band interference to an FS system receiving station located in a
third country not covered by the DC-MSS-IMT system, with varying antenna azimuths and heights;
2) DC-MSS-IMT coverage of all South American countries, including the country where the victim
FS receiving station is located. Scenarios related to DC-MSS-IMT System 3 at altitudes of 340 km
and 525 km were studied, with load factors of 20% and 50%. The results showed that compatibility
between the DC-MSS-IMT and FS systems is feasible in adjacent-band condition with contiguous
and non-contigous band conditions. An interference mitigation strategy based on operational or
service margin, implementing a form of exclusion zone at the border between countries involved in
a cross-border interference situation, as in Case (1), was presented and tested, and the results showed
that its use may be of interest in certain situations.
